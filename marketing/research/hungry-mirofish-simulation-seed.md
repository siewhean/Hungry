# Hungry Consumer Reaction Simulation Seed

## Document Purpose

This document is a reality seed for MiroFish. It describes the Hungry app, the launch positioning, the early marketing materials, and the research questions for a before-versus-after marketing simulation.

The goal is to understand how likely target users are to care about Hungry before seeing any marketing, how their perception changes after seeing the current marketing materials, what they like, what confuses them, and what should be improved in both the product and the messaging.

## Product Summary

Hungry is a dish-first food discovery app for Singapore. Instead of forcing people to start with a venue, it starts with the dish the person wants to eat and helps them identify the right nearby stall, hawker centre, or food court option.

Core idea:
- Find the dish. Not just the restaurant.
- Start from craving, then narrow by nearby relevance, taste fit, and stall context.
- Reduce lunch indecision and help people choose faster with more confidence.

Market context:
- Singapore diners often know what they want to eat before they know where to get it.
- Existing tools are usually restaurant-first or map-first.
- Hawker centres are culturally important but still hard to navigate digitally.

## Current Product State

Hungry is currently a frontend-first, mock-first mobile product concept for iOS and Android.

Implemented product themes and capabilities:
- Dish-first search
- Six-axis taste filtering: sweet, spicy, sour, salty, rich, aromatic
- Nearby discovery for hawker centres, food courts, and local dining
- Stall detail with floor and unit context
- Dish detail, reviews, favourites, and delivery links where available
- "Decide For Me" nearby recommendation flow

Product promise:
- Less time deciding
- Better odds of choosing the right dish the first time
- More useful local context for Singapore food discovery

## Problem Statement

Hungry aims to solve three connected user problems:

1. Decision fatigue
- People waste time deciding what to eat.
- Existing discovery tools are slow when the user starts with a craving.

2. Dish-first intent mismatch
- Users often think "I want laksa" before they think "I want this venue."
- Most apps still force restaurant-first browsing.

3. Hawker-centre discovery friction
- Hawker centres and food courts are dense, practical, and local.
- Existing discovery tools do a weak job of showing floor, unit, stall-level clarity, and dish-level comparison.

## Primary Audience Segments

### Segment A: Singapore office workers and daily diners

Typical profile:
- 22 to 38 years old
- Office workers, university students, or early-career professionals
- Often deciding lunch quickly near CBD, campuses, or business districts

Typical motivations:
- Choose lunch faster
- Compare nearby options without opening multiple apps
- Reduce decision fatigue during busy weekdays

Typical frustrations:
- Endless venue scrolling
- Not enough detail on what to order
- Too much effort to compare similar dishes across nearby stalls

### Segment B: Tourists and expats in Singapore

Typical profile:
- 25 to 45 years old
- Newer to Singapore food culture
- Interested in local dishes but not always confident about what to order or where to find it

Typical motivations:
- Discover local food more confidently
- Understand hawker centres and food courts
- Find dishes rather than premium venue recommendations

Typical frustrations:
- Blog articles are inspiring but not actionable
- Hawker centres are overwhelming
- Current tools do not help enough with "what should I eat here?"

### Segment C: Hawker stall owners and small operators

Typical profile:
- Stall owners who want better discovery without needing to become content creators

Typical motivations:
- Get found when people search by dish
- Reach younger diners and newcomers

Typical frustrations:
- Foot traffic is inconsistent
- Discovery is often driven by random social content
- Good dishes are still hard to find digitally

Note:
- Consumer reaction should be the primary focus of the simulation.
- Hawker-owner reaction should be a smaller secondary layer.

## Baseline Product Concept To Test Before Marketing

Before seeing any launch materials, simulated users should react only to this plain concept:

"Hungry is a mobile app for Singapore that helps people search by dish instead of only by restaurant or venue. Users can look for dishes such as laksa, chicken rice, satay, fish soup, and other local meals, compare nearby options, use taste-based filters, and find the right hawker stall or food-court option more quickly."

At this stage, users should not yet see polished launch messaging or marketing hooks. The point is to observe their raw reaction to the underlying product idea.

## Marketing Materials To Apply In The Second Phase

After the baseline reaction is captured, users should be exposed to the current Hungry marketing layer.

### Core positioning
- Find the dish. Not just the restaurant.
- Dish-first food discovery for Singapore.
- Makan nearby with more confidence.

### Hero framing
- Hungry helps you decide what to eat faster by starting with the dish you are craving, then showing you the right nearby stall, hawker centre, and taste fit.

### Main consumer proof points
- Dish-first search
- Filter by taste
- Decide faster with one strong nearby suggestion
- Hawker-centre and stall clarity
- Reviews and delivery-link context where available

### Marketing hooks used in current materials
- "You are not bad at deciding lunch. Your tools are just backwards."
- "If you already know you want laksa, why are you still starting with the restaurant?"
- "Google Maps tells you where. It does not tell you what to order."
- "Not another restaurant app. A dish-first one."

### Waitlist-style CTA
- Join the waitlist
- Get early access
- Be first to try Hungry

### Brand voice
- Warm
- Direct
- Useful
- Local
- Grounded
- Appetite-led

### Messaging guardrails
- Avoid hype-heavy startup language
- Avoid fake social proof or inflated claims
- Emphasize practical decision relief
- Keep the Singapore-local and hawker-first angle credible

## Research Questions

The simulation should focus on these questions:

### Product reaction before marketing
- Do target users care about the idea at all when described plainly?
- What immediate emotional response appears first: interest, confusion, indifference, skepticism, or excitement?
- Do users instantly understand the dish-first concept?
- What do they assume the app does well?
- What do they worry the app will do poorly?

### Reaction after marketing exposure
- Does the current messaging make the product clearer and more appealing?
- Which messages increase perceived usefulness the most?
- Which parts sound distinctive versus generic?
- Does the hawker-centre angle feel local and credible?
- Does the waitlist framing increase intent to try?

### What users like
- Which features feel most valuable?
- Which promises feel most believable?
- Which use cases sound strongest in daily life?

### What users want improved
- What still feels unclear?
- What feels missing from the product?
- What feels weak in the marketing?
- What would stop a user from joining the waitlist or trying the app?

## Simulation Design

The simulation should compare two stages:

### Stage 1: Pre-marketing concept reaction
Users only receive the plain concept description and react to the app idea itself.

### Stage 2: Post-marketing reaction
Users are then shown the current positioning, core proof points, and launch-style copy. Their reaction should be compared with Stage 1.

The analysis should identify:
- shifts in clarity
- shifts in excitement
- shifts in trust
- shifts in likelihood to try
- shifts in perceived uniqueness
- shifts in skepticism or objections

## Desired Simulation Output

The final output should answer:

1. How people feel about Hungry before marketing
2. How people feel about Hungry after marketing
3. What they like most
4. What they distrust or question
5. What should be improved in the app concept
6. What should be improved in the marketing materials
7. Which audience segments react most positively
8. Which parts of the proposition are strongest and weakest

## Decision Criteria

The simulation is useful if it produces:
- clear emotional and behavioral differences between before and after marketing
- segment-specific insights instead of generic feedback
- concrete objections and improvement ideas
- realistic feedback on whether the current launch messaging is strong enough
